Your car does fewer kilometres than advertised because that number comes from the WLTP, a laboratory
test made for comparing cars, not for predicting a trip. On the road, range is usually lower,
especially on the motorway and in the cold. To know what to count on, do the maths with the battery's
usable capacity and the real consumption of your version.

:::essencial
- **The maths is simple**: usable capacity (kWh) ÷ consumption (kWh/100 km) × 100 gives the approximate range, counting only the part of the battery you use.
- **The WLTP figure is usually 10 to 30 % above real use**, and in the cold and on the motorway range can drop by up to 40 %.
- **Speed and climate control weigh a lot**: above about 100 km/h consumption rises sharply, and heating or cooling the car while it's plugged in spares the battery.
:::

## Before you do the maths, answer four questions {#antes-de-fazer-contas}

There are no guaranteed kilometres. There's a range, and these answers tell you where you fall in it.

1. **Which car, version and year?** The usable capacity and consumption are those of that exact version.
2. **What kind of route?** Town, open road or motorway, and the speed you usually drive at.
3. **What time of year**, or what temperature? Cold and heat weigh a lot.
4. **What for?** Everyday driving or a specific trip, from where to where.

Then look up the spec sheet for your car's exact version and year. The UVE (the Portuguese EV users'
association) recommends consumption measured on the road, not just the WLTP figure. The
[EV Database](https://www.ev-database.org/), for example, gives real range and consumption by model and
version.

## The maths: kW, kWh and consumption {#as-contas}

Many manufacturers and apps talk in kilometres instead of kW and kWh. According to the UVE, the units
are simple:

- **kW (kilowatt) is power**: how fast the charger delivers energy or the motor uses it.
- **kWh (kilowatt-hour) is energy**: what the battery stores and what you pay for. Power times hours
  gives energy.
- **Consumption** is the energy the car uses per distance, in kWh/100 km.

With that, the approximate range comes from a single sum:

**Range = usable capacity (kWh) ÷ consumption (kWh/100 km) × 100**

With 40 kWh and a consumption of 20 kWh/100 km, that's about 200 km. At 16 kWh/100 km, about 250 km.
These numbers are there to show the maths, not those of any car.

It's a simplification. In everyday driving you don't go from 100 % to 0 %: for usable range, count only
the part of the battery you use, from 80 % to 10 %, for example.

**To compare chargers, look at the kW.** "Kilometres per hour of charging" depend on the car's
consumption: the same charger gives more kilometres to a more efficient car.

## Why you do fewer kilometres than advertised {#anunciada-e-real}

The range the manufacturer advertises comes from the WLTP, a laboratory test. According to the UVE,
the cycle covers about 23 km in 30 minutes, at an average of 46.5 km/h, with a maximum of 131 km/h and
about 23 °C. A motorway in winter is another story.

That's why real range is usually lower, especially on the motorway and in the cold. The WLTP figure is
usually 10 to 30 % above real use.

:::aviso
**Don't plan a trip on the brochure number.** In the cold and on the motorway, real range can drop by up to 40 %. Count on consumption measured on the road and use a route-planning app for electric cars.
:::

None of this means the manufacturer is cheating. The test is for comparing cars with each other, not
for predicting your trip.

## WLTP, EPA and CLTC: one car, three numbers {#wltp-epa-e-cltc}

In the United States and China, range is measured with other tests. All three are laboratory tests: useful for
comparing cars, not for predicting a specific trip. And they can't be compared with each other without
converting.

| | WLTP | EPA | CLTC |
|---|---|---|---|
| Where | Europe and many other markets | United States | China |
| Against real use | Usually 10 to 30 % above | The most realistic: usually off by just a few percentage points | The most optimistic of the three |

### WLTP: the European one {#wltp}

The Worldwide Harmonised Light Vehicles Test Procedure has been the test in Europe, and in many other
markets, since 2019. It replaced the NEDC.

- **In a laboratory**, on a dynamometer, at 23 °C, with no passengers or accessories, and no air
  conditioning or heating.
- **A 30-minute cycle** with four intensity phases: low (up to 60 km/h), medium (up to 80), high (up to
  100) and extra-high (above 130). A computer drives the car until the battery runs out.
- **A single number.** The WLTP combines the phases into one combined figure, and the advertised range
  is the result of that test. It doesn't publish separate town and motorway figures, as the EPA does.

### EPA: the American one, closest to real use {#epa}

The cycle of the Environmental Protection Agency, in the United States, is the closest to real use.

- **Five tests**: town, motorway and more aggressive high-speed driving, all three without air
  conditioning; a hot one (95 °F), with the air conditioning cooling; and a cold one (20 °F), with
  heating and demisting.
- **Until the battery runs out**, in single-cycle tests (town and motorway) or multi-cycle ones (four
  town, two motorway and two at constant speed).
- **A factor of 0.7** applied to every parameter, range included, to approximate real use: aggressive
  driving, air conditioning, cold.
- **Three figures, one on the label.** There's town, motorway and combined. The combined one weights
  town at 55 % and motorway at 45 %, and it's the only one on the label.

### CLTC: the Chinese one, the most optimistic {#cltc}

The China Light-duty Vehicle Test Cycle is the Chinese test.

- **Three phases**: low speed (CLTC-P1), medium (CLTC-P2) and high (CLTC-P3).
- **Town routes**: 11 short trips and 12 stops, with an average of 29 km/h, a maximum of 114 km/h and
  22 % of the time idling.
- **Built around congested town traffic**, at low speed. For motorway use, take about 30 % off the
  CLTC number.

### From one test to another {#conversao}

- **From EPA to WLTP**, multiply by 1.12 to 1.15. In other words, WLTP ≈ EPA × 1.14.
- **The CLTC** is usually about 35 % above the EPA, and a few points above the NEDC, which is now
  obsolete.

These are rules of thumb. The real difference varies from manufacturer to manufacturer and from model
to model.

## Five things that cut your range {#o-que-reduz-a-autonomia}

The UVE points to these five factors. See which ones apply to you:

- **Speed.** Above about 100 km/h, consumption rises sharply. In town, low speed and regenerative
  braking help: it's normal for the car to do more kilometres in town than on the motorway.
- **Driving style.** Hard acceleration and heavy braking use more.
- **Route.** Climbs use a lot. On the way down, the car recovers part of the energy.
- **Climate control.** Heating or cooling the cabin uses battery energy, even more so in the cold.
- **Weight.** Luggage and passengers increase consumption.

The why: high speed increases air resistance. Cold reduces the battery's capacity, and heating uses
energy. Heat means the battery has to be cooled.

## How to get more kilometres from the same battery {#como-poupar-energia}

This is the UVE's advice:

- **Heat or cool the car while it's plugged in.** Do it before you leave, with energy from the grid
  rather than the battery. Many cars let you schedule this in the app.
- **Use the heated seats and steering wheel**, if the car has them. They use less than heating the
  whole cabin.
- **Anticipate your braking.** Lifting off the accelerator earlier lets regenerative braking return
  energy to the battery, and wears the brakes and tyres less.
- **Get to know the regeneration modes.** Many cars have a B mode, paddles on the steering wheel or
  one-pedal driving, which brake more with the motor. Find out how they work on your car, and what the
  ECO mode does, if it has one.
- **Give it time.** Your driving adapts with habit: after a few months, it's normal to use less.

## On a trip, plan stops up to 80 % {#viagens}

Use a route-planning app for electric cars. According to the UVE, given the car and the charge at
departure, the app tells you where to stop, how long to charge and what charge you'll arrive with. And
it keeps updating during the trip.

Fast charging slows down above about 80 %. So, on a trip, plan your stops up to 80 %.

## Myths and mistakes {#mitos-e-enganos}

- **"The car should do its advertised range."** It shouldn't, and the manufacturer isn't cheating. The
  number comes from the WLTP, a laboratory test at about 23 °C and an average of 46.5 km/h, according
  to the UVE. It's for comparing cars, and it's usually 10 to 30 % above real use.
- **"You use more in town than on the motorway."** In an electric car, it's the other way round. Low
  speed and regenerative braking help, and it's normal to do more kilometres in town, according to the
  UVE.
- **"What you use going up, you get back coming down."** Only part of it. Climbs use a lot, and on the
  way down the car recovers part of the energy, the UVE explains.
- **"Kilometres per hour of charging measure the charger."** They measure the car: they depend on its
  consumption, and the same charger gives more kilometres to a more efficient car. To compare chargers, use the kW.
- **"A CLTC range is worth the same as a WLTP one."** It isn't. The CLTC is the most optimistic of the
  three tests: for the motorway, take about 30 % off it.
- **"On a trip, always charge to 100 %."** Fast charging slows down above about 80 %. Plan your stops
  up to 80 %.
- **"Warming the car up before you leave drains the battery."** Not if it's plugged in: the energy
  comes from the grid, not the battery, according to the UVE.
