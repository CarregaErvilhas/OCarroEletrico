You can charge your car from a domestic socket, and the law allows it. If you charge every day, a
wallbox on its own circuit is faster and safer. And in a building, your parking space is yours: the
condominium (condomínio) is told, it doesn't grant permission, and it can only object in five
specific cases.

:::essencial
- **Domestic socket**: fine if you charge little, at 10 A at most on an ordinary Schuko, with the circuit checked by an electrician.
- **Wallbox**: the choice for every day, on a dedicated circuit with its own residual-current device, fitted by an installer registered with the DGEG, the energy authority.
- **In a building**: if the installation touches common parts, notify the building's management in writing 30 days ahead. No reasoned objection by then, and you go ahead.
:::

## Before you ask for a quote, answer six questions {#antes-de-pedir-orcamento}

These answers decide what you need. Have them ready when you talk to the electrician.

1. **House or apartment building?** In a building: a closed garage that's only yours, a space in the shared garage, or the street?
2. **Which car, exact version?** What matters is the on-board charger's AC power, its phases and the plug.
3. **How many kilometres do you drive a day**, on average? That's what tells you whether a domestic socket is enough.
4. **What contracted power do you have**, and is the contract single-phase or three-phase? It's on your electricity bill.
5. **Which tariff?** Flat, two-period or three-period.
6. **Do you have solar panels?** If so, you have self-consumption (a UPAC).

## Domestic socket: enough if you charge little {#tomada-domestica}

The law allows charging from a domestic socket in places not open to the public, as long as the
technical and safety rules are met (DL 93/2025, art. 21.º, n.º 2). It's the slowest option. It works
when the car stays plugged in for many hours and the day's kilometres are few.

Before the first charge, get an electrician to check the circuit. Charging a car asks a socket for a
lot of current for hours on end, and the circuit has to cope.

:::aviso
**10 A, no more.** On an ordinary Schuko, the DGEG limits charging current to 10 A, about 2.3 kW. Only a socket factory-marked for charging vehicles, on a dedicated circuit, takes 16 A (3.7 kW). Many cables let you choose the current: on an ordinary Schuko, set 10 A or less.
:::

- **The cable that came with the car is a backup**, not the way to charge every day for a long time.
  If the socket will be your daily solution, the UVE (the Portuguese EV users' association)
  recommends a reinforced socket and the installation checked by a certified electrician.
- **For new electrical work, ask for a socket made for charging.** The DGEG's technical guide only
  recommends domestic sockets in installations that already exist.
- **What the electrician must comply with**, according to the DGEG guide: a dedicated circuit for each
  car, rated for at least 16 A, with cables of 2.5 mm² or more; and its own residual-current device
  (RCD) of 30 mA or less, at least type A, for each connection point.
- **No extension leads, no multi-plugs, no second cable.** Adapters only if they're made for charging
  vehicles, marked as such and approved by the makers of the car and of the equipment.

## Wallbox: for charging every day {#wallbox}

A wallbox charges faster and more safely than a domestic socket. It lets you schedule charging and, on
many models, adjusts its power to what the house has spare at each moment (dynamic load balancing).

**The power you'll get is the lowest of three**: the wallbox's, the car's on-board AC charger's, and
what the installation and your contracted power leave free.

- **The on-board charger is the boss.** It ranges from 3.7 kW, common in plug-in hybrids, to 22 kW,
  according to the UVE. Check the one in your car's exact version before choosing: a wallbox more
  powerful than it doesn't charge any faster.
- **Three-phase only with a three-phase contract.** A three-phase wallbox on a single-phase contract
  means changing the contract.
- **More contracted power means a higher fixed charge** on every bill. If the house's or building's
  supply line has power available, you just ask your electricity supplier. If it doesn't, the upgrade
  is work for E-REDES, the grid operator, and it can be expensive. In a building, according to the UVE,
  the owner who asks for it pays, unless there's an agreement with the condominium.
- **Protection against DC fault currents.** A wallbox with a Type 2 socket or cable needs a type B RCD,
  or a type A or F one with a DC detector, unless the wallbox already has that protection built in.
  Check with the installer.

### Portable charger or wallbox? {#carregador-portatil-ou-wallbox}

Both are EVSE, the equipment that connects the car to the electricity. Neither charges the battery:
the car's on-board charger does that.

| | Portable charger (mobile EVSE) | Wallbox (fixed EVSE) |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | A cable with safety electronics that connects a socket to the car | A unit fixed to the wall, on a dedicated circuit |
| Power | The socket's: about 2.3 kW on an ordinary Schuko, up to 22 kW on a red 32 A CEE socket | Up to the limit of the on-board charger and the installation |
| Extras | Overcurrent protection, leakage detection, controlled start | Dynamic load balancing, schedules, metering and, on many models, a solar mode |
| What for | Trips, a holiday home, a backup | Everyday charging |

The portable one isn't a "dumb" cable, but it depends on the socket it finds and that socket's
protection. A wallbox has a circuit and protection built to measure.

### With solar panels {#com-paineis-solares}

With self-consumption, the car can charge from surplus solar during the day, and some wallboxes have a
solar mode that follows production. It only pays off if the car is home while the sun is up. If it's
only there at night, the off-peak tariff matters more than the panels.

## Who installs it and what paperwork to ask for {#quem-instala-e-que-papeis-pedir}

Installing a charger at home falls under the electrical installations regime (DL 96/2017), by way of
DL 93/2025, art. 25.º, n.º 2. The installer works to the DGEG's Technical Guide for supplying electric
vehicles, 3rd edition, from 2023. Portaria n.º 128/2026/1 (art. 3.º, n.º 4) provides for a new guide,
but in November 2026 the DGEG still points to the 3rd edition.

- **Who can install it**: an installation company (entidade instaladora, EI) or a technician
  responsible for the work, both registered with the DGEG. A technician alone can take on
  installations up to 41.4 kVA; above that, only an EI (Lei n.º 14/2015, art. 4.º, n.º 3; Portaria n.º
  128/2026/1, art. 4.º, n.º 1). Ask for their DGEG registration number.
- **Whether it needs a design project** depends on the type of installation (DL 96/2017, art. 5.º).
  According to the DGEG, it's required, among other cases, for type B installations and for type C ones
  with more than 10.35 kVA supplied by the grid. Whether yours needs one is the installer's call.
- **At the end, insist on the paperwork.** The installer hands over a declaration of conformity of the
  work or a statement of responsibility for it. Keep it: you'll need it for insurance, for the
  condominium and to sell the house.
- **After that, it's on you.** Someone with a charger that isn't public is called a DPC, and the law
  requires them to keep up its technical and safety conditions and to use equipment that meets the
  standards (Portaria n.º 128/2026/1, art. 4.º, n.º 2).

The technical rules in detail (charging modes, circuit, RCD, which board feeds each space, power in
buildings) are in [electrical installation](/instalacao-eletrica/).

## In a building: the space is yours, and so are the rules {#num-predio}

In a house, or in a garage wired directly to your own unit's installation without going through
common parts, there's nothing to tell the condominium, according to the UVE. The rest of this section
is for when the installation touches common parts.

### The right to install {#o-direito-de-instalar}

Any owner in the building can install, at their own cost, a charger in their own parking space, as
long as the space meets the technical requirements set by ordinance (DL 93/2025, art. 23.º, n.º 1).
The same goes for a socket. According to the DGEG, tenants and lawful occupants have the same right:
n.º 3, a), refers to them, and n.º 2 requires the owner to be notified too.

### Which board the electricity comes from {#de-que-quadro-sai-a-eletricidade}

Exclusive or shared space, and which electrical board feeds it: that's what decides who is responsible
for the charger (DL 93/2025, art. 21.º, n.º 1, as the DGEG reads it).

| Space | Can be fed from | Who operates the charger |
|---|---|---|
| Exclusive use (a box or marked on the floor), wired to the unit's installation | The unit's main board | You, as a DPC |
| Exclusive use, wired to a shared board | The riser board, the common services board or the garage's | The condominium, or an OPC (charge point operator) it hires |
| Shared use | The riser board or the common services board | The condominium, or an OPC it hires |

The DGEG's rules for the route:

- **Wired to your unit, it comes from the main board**, not a secondary one (the upstairs board of a
  duplex, for example). The same applies to shops and offices in the same building.
- **Cables run through common areas and your own unit's spaces**, never through another owner's space.
- **In a space marked on the floor**, the circuit comes from a board of its own that only opens with a
  tool, with a main switch near the access.

:::aviso
**Cables can't run through the lift shaft**, or through ducts or pipes of other installations. That's the DGEG's position, citing the RTIEBT. If the proposed route goes that way, the DGEG doesn't allow it. They can run in the riser duct, under conditions.
:::

The standard layouts for each case, and why the DGEG prefers the riser board to the common services
one, are in [electrical installation](/instalacao-eletrica/#de-que-quadro-se-alimenta).

### Tell the building 30 days ahead {#comunicacao-previa}

If the installation sits in a common part or goes through one, even for a space that's only yours,
notice is mandatory. It goes in writing to the condominium's management, and also to the owner if
you're a tenant, at least **30 days** before the installation date (art. 23.º, n.º 2).

What to put in it: who you are and which space, the equipment and its power, where the cables run
through common parts, who's installing (a certified installer) and the planned date. The UVE has a
[ready-to-use template](https://www.uve.pt/page/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/2025_Minuta_ponto_de_carga.docx),
in Portuguese.

- **Keep proof** of the notice and of the date it was received: registered letter with acknowledgement
  of receipt, or an email with confirmation. It's what starts the 30 days.
- **Get a technical visit first**, the UVE recommends: a certified electrician or charger installer
  looks at the space before you choose the equipment. A proposal that works from the start avoids a
  refusal and starting over.
- **The more detail, the better.** Many owners don't know electric cars and have fears a well-done
  installation doesn't justify. A clear description answers those fears before they reach the meeting.

### The building can only say no in five cases {#quando-o-condominio-se-pode-opor}

The law lists them, one by one (art. 23.º, n.º 3):

- **a)** the condominium installs, **within 90 days**, one or more shared chargers with the same
  services and technology, covering the needs of every potential user;
- **b)** the building already has a shared charger or socket meeting those conditions;
- **c)** the installation demonstrably puts people or property at risk, with a technical opinion
  delivered to the management. According to the DGEG, that opinion comes from an electrical engineer or
  engineering technician registered with the DGEG and, if fire safety could be affected, also from a
  fire safety (SCIE) technician. A worry without an opinion isn't enough;
- **d)** the installation gets in the way of the common access routes;
- **e)** it breaks the accessibility rules in the common spaces (DL 163/2006).

Outside those five, there is no "no".

### Deadlines and the decision {#prazos-e-decisao}

- **30 days to decide**, at most, from the notice (art. 23.º, n.º 4). If the management decides, it
  needs a simple majority of the building's total value. According to the UVE, that's the majority of
  ownership shares (permilagem), not the majority of votes of those at the meeting.
- **It goes in the minutes**, is sent in writing to absent owners within 15 days and, if negative, must
  give reasons (art. 23.º, n.ºs 4 e 5).
- **No reasoned objection in time, you go ahead.** The right to install is the rule; objection is the
  exception, and only counts in one of the five cases and within the 30 days. If the management doesn't
  tell you such a reason in time, the installation can go ahead on the date you gave.
- **If the condominium disputes it later**, get legal support. The UVE offers it to its members.

### Who pays {#quem-paga}

The installation is paid by whoever asks for it, the owner (art. 23.º, n.º 1). The energy can come
from the unit's installation or from a delivery point of its own, the sub-CPE, under the terms set by
ERSE, the energy regulator (art. 24.º, n.º 3). [When a sub-CPE pays off, and how to ask for one](/sub-cpe/).

### New buildings {#predios-novos}

New buildings under horizontal property, or with parking, must come with electrical infrastructure
ready for charging vehicles, with a minimum power set by ordinance. Chargers and sockets aren't
included (DL 93/2025, art. 22.º). According to the DGEG, Portaria n.º 128/2026/1 (art. 2.º) sets the
minimums:

- each connection point has at least 3680 VA;
- in a residential building, only some spaces must be prepared: N = 0.8 + 0.2 × n, rounded up, where n
  is the number of spaces in the car park not counting those already fed by the units. In a 30-space
  car park, that's 7;
- in a residential building a load control system is mandatory, cutting or reducing charging when
  there isn't enough power;
- the cost is the developer's (art. 4.º, n.º 3).

In other words: **in a new building, don't count on a connection at every space.** Check with the
management or in the project.

## On MOBI.E or not {#ligado-a-mobi-e-ou-nao}

Until 31 December 2027, the MOBI.E platform, Portugal's public charging network, is still managed by
the EGME, the electric mobility managing body (DL 93/2025, art. 44.º, as amended by DL 155/2026). A
private charger can be connected to it or not.

- **Not connected**, the car charges with the house's electricity, at your contract's price.
- **Connected to MOBI.E**, charging goes through the platform and pays the EGME tariff set by ERSE,
  which must be itemised on the bill if passed on to you (art. 44.º, n.º 4).
- Some [grants for chargers in condominiums](/incentivos/) require the MOBI.E connection.

How a wallbox connected to MOBI.E works in this transition period, according to the UVE:

- **You start charging with a card or app from a CEME**, the company that sells you energy on the
  network, as at a public charger. You pay no OPC fee, because the charger is yours: you pay for the
  energy at the price of the card you use. With a fleet card, the invoice goes to the company.
- **The wallbox's consumption is kept separate** from the rest of the house or building, and each gets
  its own bill. That's what makes this option useful on a space wired to the common services board.
- **It only charges with a card**, and you can limit it to the cards you choose. A socket can't make
  that distinction.

And after 2027? Nobody knows yet: the CEME transition period ends on 31 December 2027, and the new
regime's regulations don't say how a connected wallbox will work. Be wary of anyone who promises you
otherwise.

## What charging at home costs {#quanto-custa}

It depends on three things: the price per kWh in your home contract, the time period you charge in
(off-peak or not) and, if the charger is on MOBI.E, the EGME tariff and the supplier's price.

On a two-period or three-period tariff, the rule is simple: **schedule charging for off-peak hours.**
To do the maths for your case, use the tools at the end of the page. If you're looking for money for
the charger or the car, see the [grants in force](/incentivos/).

## Sockets and plugs {#tomadas-e-fichas}

At home, the socket is the **Schuko (Type F)**, 230 V and 16 A, with the 10 A limit for charging
explained [above](/carregar-em-casa/#tomada-domestica). On the car's side, there are two AC plugs:

- **Type 2 (Mennekes)**: the European standard, single-phase or three-phase, up to 43 kW. It's the plug
  on most cars sold in Europe and on wallboxes.
- **Type 1 (J1772)**: the North American standard, single-phase, up to 7.7 kW. Only found on cars
  imported from the US or Canada, and needs an adapter or a wallbox with a Type 1 cable.

Industrial CEE sockets (blue and red), DC plugs (CCS, CHAdeMO) and those from other countries are in
[charging on the road](/carregamento-publico/#fichas-e-tomadas). The installation rules for each socket (standard,
height, switch next to the socket) are in [electrical installation](/instalacao-eletrica/#tomadas-e-fichas).

## V2L, V2H and V2G: when the car gives energy back {#v2l-v2h-e-v2g}

Bidirectional charging puts the car's battery to work powering other things. There are four kinds:

- **V2L (Vehicle-to-Load)**: the car powers appliances through a socket on the car itself, or an
  adapter. No wallbox or special installation needed.
- **V2H (Vehicle-to-Home)**: the car powers the house. It needs a bidirectional wallbox and a certified
  electrical installation.
- **V2G (Vehicle-to-Grid)**: the car sends energy to the public grid, with a contract and payment. It
  needs a compatible car, a bidirectional wallbox, software, an energy contract, the grid operator's
  approval and a bidirectional meter.
- **V2B (Vehicle-to-Building)**: a variant of V2G for buildings, without feeding into the public grid.

### What the law says {#v2x-o-que-diz-a-lei}

- DL 93/2025 (art. 2.º) defines bidirectional charging: smart charging in which electricity can flow
  the other way, from the battery to the charging point.
- Art. 6.º of the same decree-law requires charging points installed or renewed to allow ad hoc and
  smart charging under AFIR, Regulation (EU) 2023/1804. The communication standards for bidirectional
  charging are AFIR's.
- ERSE's Electric Mobility Regulation governs what OPCs and eMSPs do, including metering and consumption
  data. The transitional regime runs until 31 December 2027 (DL 155/2026).
- Using smart or bidirectional charging to provide services to the grid needs no new rules beyond
  those that already exist.

### Where things stand {#v2x-onde-estamos}

- **The law is ready, the cars aren't yet.** Fewer than 10 % of electric models in production
  supported bidirectional charging in 2026. Most new cars only charge one way.
- **There's no consolidated European standard** for bidirectional AC or DC yet, and interoperability is
  the main obstacle.
- **In Portugal, V2H has an open door** through DL 93/2025, via self-consumption. Commercial V2G is
  still marginal.

The standards you'll hear about:

- **ISO 15118-20**: the car-to-charger communication that supports bidirectional charging and Plug &
  Charge. ISO 15118-2 is the first generation, one way only.
- **OCPP**: the protocol between the charger and the system that manages it.
- **IEC 61851** and **IEC 62196**: conductive charging, and plugs and sockets.
- **EN 50549** and the European grid codes (RfG and DCC): the rules for connecting to the grid, still
  being consolidated for V2G.

## Myths and mistakes {#mitos-e-enganos}

- **"The building has to give permission."** It doesn't. The right to install is yours (art. 23.º,
  n.º 1). The condominium can only object in one of the law's five cases, and has 30 days to do it.
- **"It takes a two-thirds vote and the building has 60 days."** Those were the rules of the previous
  regime, now revoked (DL 39/2010, art. 29.º), like the "architectural line" argument. Today: a simple
  majority of the building's total value and 30 days.
- **"A more powerful wallbox charges faster."** Only up to the limit of the car's on-board charger.
  Beyond that, you gain nothing.
- **"The cable that came with the car is fine for every day."** It's an occasional charger, made as a
  backup. For every day, a wallbox, or at least a reinforced socket checked by a certified electrician.
- **"The wallbox charges the battery."** The car's on-board charger charges the battery. The wallbox
  and the cable just deliver the energy safely.
- **"In a new building, every space already has a connection."** Not necessarily. The law only requires
  some spaces to be prepared. Check before you buy the wallbox.
