It depends on the chemistry. An NMC battery is best kept in a middle range day to day, with 100 %
saved for when you need the full range; an LFP one can, and as a rule should, go to 100 % regularly.
Whichever you have, the manufacturer's manual is the boss, and on a used car the number that counts
is the battery's state of health (SoH).

:::essencial
- **NMC**: day to day, keep the charge in a middle range (the UVE, the Portuguese EV users' association, says about 80 % at most and 10 % at least), with 100 % only when you need it, like before a trip.
- **LFP**: it can, and as a rule should, charge to 100 % regularly, because that's how the car calibrates its state-of-charge estimate.
- **On a used car**: before you buy, ask for an independent battery report and check how much of the battery warranty is left.
:::

## First, get to know your battery {#antes-de-comecar}

Your car manufacturer's instructions come first. The rest are general trends, not absolute rules,
as the UVE stresses.

And nobody can promise you how many years or kilometres a battery will last. According to the UVE,
degradation depends on six things: the chemistry, the temperature, the power you charge at, the depth
of the cycles, the time the battery spends at a high charge and the car's thermal management. Be wary
of anyone who guarantees you a number.

To know which rules apply to you, answer three questions:

1. **Which car, version and year?** The chemistry (LFP or NMC), the usable capacity and the warranty are on the spec sheet for that exact version and year. The same model can have LFP or NMC batteries depending on the version.
2. **How do you charge?** Mostly at home or at work, or mostly at fast chargers?
3. **How much do you drive?** Your daily kilometres, and whether you often take long trips.

## NMC or LFP: the chemistry decides how far to charge {#no-dia-a-dia}

- **NMC battery: in the middle, day to day.** Keep the charge in a middle range and save 100 % for
  when you need the full range, for example before a trip. Spending a long time at a high charge
  speeds up degradation, the UVE explains. Based on its members' experience, the UVE recommends
  charging to about 80 % and not letting it drop below 10 %.
- **Use the charge limit.** Many cars let you cap the maximum charge. With an NMC battery, that's the
  setting to turn on.
- **LFP battery: to 100 %, regularly.** It can, and as a rule should, charge to 100 % regularly. That's
  how the car calibrates its state-of-charge estimate, according to the UVE.

## 0 % on the dashboard isn't empty, but don't go looking for it {#nao-chegar-a-vazio}

According to VOLT (Exame Informática), the 0 % the dashboard shows isn't 0 % in the cells, because
the manufacturer keeps a safety margin. But running down to very low levels shortens the battery's life.

There's no exact number for the minimum. The UVE's manual points to charging again at around 20 to
25 %.

:::aviso
**Below 10 %, charge now.** Left parked with a very low charge, the car can end up with unbalanced cells. Then what the BMS (the system that manages the battery) shows no longer matches the real charge. And in a voltage drop there may not be enough charge to start up, which can drain the 12 V battery and stop the high-voltage system from working.
:::

## Fast charging for trips, AC for every day {#carregamentos-rapidos}

Fast charging every day, back to back, can degrade the battery faster. For everyday use, the UVE
advises charging on AC (alternating current): at home, at work or at a normal charger (posto normal).
Fast chargers are for trips.

No need for alarm: according to the UVE, that wear has only been seen in extreme cases, like taxis
and ride-hailing (TVDE).

How to set up charging at home is in [socket or wallbox](/carregar-em-casa/). Public chargers, and
how long charging at them takes, are in [charging on the road](/carregamento-publico/).

## In the cold, LFP loses more {#frio-e-calor}

In the cold, LFP batteries lose more available capacity and more charging power than NMC ones,
according to the UVE.

- **Precondition the battery before a fast charge.** It helps keep the power up. Check whether your
  car has this feature and how to turn it on.
- **Stay away from extremes.** Whenever you can, don't leave the car in the sun or in extreme cold.
  The UVE's manual suggests keeping it and charging it in a garage, or charging at night in summer.

What changes the kilometres you get from each charge is in [range](/autonomia/).

## A car parked for a long time: leave it in the middle {#carro-parado}

For a car that will sit parked for a long time, VOLT advises leaving the battery at around 50 %.
And also:

- **Don't leave it plugged into the charger at 100 % for days**, unless it has the charge limit
  turned on.
- **If it was left at a high charge**, above 70 to 80 %, use a little before parking it.
- **Check the level after a few days.** The battery loses charge even when parked.
- **And never leave it nearly empty**: [below 10 %, charge now](/baterias/#nao-chegar-a-vazio).

## LFP or NMC: neither is better, it's a trade-off {#lfp-ou-nmc}

According to the UVE, neither chemistry is better than the other. Each wins at some things and loses
at others:

| | LFP | NMC |
|---|---|---|
| Density | Lower: more weight and volume for the same energy | Higher: more range in the same space |
| Charging to 100 % | It can and should, regularly | Only when needed |
| Cycles | Very resistant to heavy use | Less so |
| Cold | Worse | Better |
| Cost | As a rule cheaper | As a rule more expensive |

- **Few kilometres a day, and you charge at home or at work?** An LFP is enough and simple to live
  with.
- **Lots of long trips, or lots of range in a small car?** An NMC can pay off.
- **Capacity alone is misleading.** A 60 kWh LFP and a 60 kWh NMC don't behave the same. And
  manufacturers don't let you use all the physical capacity: they keep a safety margin.
- **For trips, the charging curve matters more** (how the power changes over the charge) than the
  maximum power advertised.

## The warranty isn't just the years {#garantia}

The market standard is 8 years or 160,000 km, but always check the warranty for your car's exact
version and year. And read all of it, not just the years, as the UVE recommends. Look at three
things:

- **the minimum capacity guaranteed at the end of the period**, the minimum SoH;
- **the kilometre limit**;
- **the conditions and limits of use.**

**Professional use is another story.** On a ride-hailing (TVDE) car, for example, the warranty can
have stricter conditions or shorter terms.

## On a used car, what counts is the SoH {#carro-usado}

On a used car's battery, the most important indicator is its state of health (SoH): how much capacity
it keeps compared with the original, according to the UVE.

:::aviso
**Before you buy, ask for a battery report.** There are independent companies that test the battery and hand over a certified report. Also check how much of the battery warranty is left.
:::

## After the car, the battery still has a use {#fim-de-vida}

A battery that no longer works well in a car can still store energy in a building, for example with
solar panels. Only after that is it recycled, according to the UVE.

## Myths and mistakes {#mitos-e-enganos}

- **"Charging to 100 % ruins the battery."** It depends on the chemistry. An LFP can, and as a rule
  should, go to 100 % regularly, according to the UVE. On an NMC, save 100 % for when you need the
  full range.
- **"0 % on the dashboard means the battery is empty."** It doesn't: the manufacturer keeps a safety
  margin. But running down to very low levels shortens the battery's life, according to VOLT.
- **"Fast charging wrecks the battery."** According to the UVE, that wear has only been seen in
  extreme cases, like taxis and ride-hailing. AC for every day, fast charging for trips.
- **"60 kWh is 60 kWh."** An LFP and an NMC with the same capacity don't behave the same. And
  manufacturers don't let you use all the physical capacity.
- **"On a trip, peak power is what counts."** The charging curve matters more than the advertised
  peak.
- **"The warranty is 8 years, end of story."** The UVE advises reading all of it. Check the minimum SoH at the end of the period, the
  kilometre limit and the conditions of use. For professional use, it can be stricter.
