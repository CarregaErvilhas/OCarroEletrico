To charge on the road, a card or an app is enough, and at chargers installed or renewed you don't
even need that: they must accept ad hoc payment, with no contract. Until 31 December 2027 two models
live side by side, the old one, MOBI.E's, and the new one, under DL 93/2025, and the price and the
invoice change from one to the other. If the charger fails, the one who answers is the OPC, the
company that operates it, not MOBI.E.

:::essencial
- **Which charger**: day to day, a normal charger while the car is parked anyway; on a trip, a rapid one up to about 80 %, then on to the next.
- **How to pay**: a CEME card or app on MOBI.E, a contract with the OPC or with an eMSP in the new model, or ad hoc, by bank card or QR code.
- **If it goes wrong**: call the OPC's support line, which is on the charger's label. Invoices and prices go to ERSE, the energy regulator; dangerous or unlicensed chargers, to ENSE, the energy sector authority.
:::

## Before you set off, answer four questions {#antes-de-sair}

The answers tell you which charger to look for and how you'll pay.

1. **Where will you charge?** In town, on a trip (from where to where) or abroad.
2. **Which car, exact version?** What matters is the plugs, the maximum AC and DC power and the charging curve.
3. **How do you pay today?** A CEME card or app, an OPC's or an eMSP's app, or nothing (ad hoc).
4. **Are you in a hurry?** The cheapest charger is often a normal one, and slow.

Prices and times aren't something you know by heart. The price of a charge is worked out with the
tools at the end of the page. The time, with the specs of the car's exact version: the maximum AC and
DC power and the charging curve.

## Normal or rapid charger: the difference is the current {#tipos-de-posto}

According to the UVE, the Portuguese EV users' association, there are three kinds of charger on the
road:

- **Normal charger**: up to 22 kW, in alternating current (AC), almost always with a Type 2 socket. At
  many you have to bring your own cable. The speed is limited by the on-board charger, the part of the
  car that converts alternating current for the battery.
- **Rapid charger**: above 22 kW, in direct current (DC), with the cable fixed to the charger. The
  usual plug is CCS; some older cars use CHAdeMO. Some chargers have both, plus an AC socket.
- **Ultra-rapid charger**: a rapid charger above 150 kW.

The UVE calls them PCN (posto de carregamento normal), PCR (posto de carregamento rápido) and PCUR
(posto de carregamento ultrarrápido).

- **At a rapid charger, use the DC plug.** If the charger also has a Type 2 socket, leave it: through
  it the car charges in AC, limited by the on-board charger. Rapid charging goes through CCS or
  CHAdeMO.
- **Tesla network.** Many Superchargers have opened to cars of other brands, and at those chargers
  Teslas have no priority, the UVE points out. Before you go, check in the Tesla app whether that
  charger accepts other brands.

### The official names don't match the UVE's {#nomes-oficiais}

In official documents, the categories are AFIR's, Regulation (EU) 2023/1804 (art. 2.º and annex). The
DGEG, the energy authority, sums them up like this:

| Current | Name | Maximum power |
|---|---|---|
| AC | slow, single-phase | under 7.4 kW |
| AC | medium speed, three-phase | from 7.4 to 22 kW |
| AC | fast, three-phase | over 22 kW |
| DC | slow | under 50 kW |
| DC | fast | from 50 kW to under 150 kW |
| DC | ultra-fast, level 1 | from 150 kW to under 350 kW |
| DC | ultra-fast, level 2 | 350 kW or more |

The limits don't match the UVE's acronyms: in AFIR, "fast" DC starts at 50 kW and "ultra-fast" at
150 kW. And up to 22 kW AFIR speaks of "normal power"; above that, "high power".

## How to pay: two models until 2027, plus ad hoc {#como-pagar}

The regime is in transition. DL 155/2026 extended the transitional period until 31 December 2027, and
until then two models coexist. The old one works with a CEME and the MOBI.E network, Portugal's public
charging network. The new one, under DL 93/2025, works with a direct contract with the OPC or with an
eMSP. The price and the invoice depend on which model the charger is in.

### Old model: one CEME card for all of MOBI.E {#modelo-antigo}

The CEME is the company that sells you energy on the MOBI.E network. You sign up with one, and it
gives you a card or an app that works at every charger on the network. According to the UVE, the
CEME's energy price is the same at every charger.

- **The OPC charges separately for using the charger**: per kWh, per minute, a fixed amount per
  charge (the activation fee) or a mix of these, the UVE explains. Those amounts must be displayed at
  the charger.
- **By the minute, your car counts.** When there's a per-minute charge, the real power the car
  charges at counts towards the price.
- **The CEME's invoice itemises everything**: the CEME's energy, the electric mobility grid access
  tariff (TAR), the OPC's fee, the fee of the EGME, MOBI.E's managing body, and the taxes (IEC excise
  duty and IVA, VAT).

### New model: a contract with the OPC or with an eMSP {#modelo-novo}

In the DL 93/2025 model, the OPC buys the electricity and sets a total price, per kWh or per kWh plus
minute. According to the UVE, the invoice is simplified, without breakdowns. The law, though, is clear: the
OPC's invoice must show the total price and the parts by type of service (DL 93/2025,
art. 14.º, n.º 5).

- **To charge on an OPC's network**, you sign up with that OPC and use its card or app.
- **An eMSP brings together chargers from several OPCs** in a single contract and a single invoice.
  The eMSP, a mobility service provider, sells you charging by card, app or subscription. Its app only
  shows the chargers it covers.
- **A subscription can change the bill a lot.** The UVE found cases where, without a subscription, you
  pay two to three times more. If you often charge on the same network, compare with and without.
- **The OPC can't discriminate.** It can't charge different prices to someone paying
  ad hoc and someone coming through an eMSP, or between eMSPs. It can give discounts on objective
  criteria, for example to subscribers (DL 93/2025, art. 14.º, n.º 4).

### Ad hoc: no contract, by bank card or QR code {#ad-hoc}

Charging ad hoc means paying for that one charge only, with no registration and no contract. Chargers
installed or renewed must allow it, under AFIR (DL 93/2025, art. 6.º, n.º 1). In practice, according
to the UVE, you pay by bank card at chargers of 50 kW or more and by QR code at lower-power ones.

**There's an exception until 1 January 2027.** It covers public chargers of 50 kW or more that were
already installed and working on 19 August 2025. At those, the obligations on means of payment,
including ad hoc, only apply from 1 January 2027 (DL 93/2025, art. 44.º, n.º 6). Until then, one of
those chargers may still not accept ad hoc payment.

:::aviso
**A bank card can be pre-authorised for much more than the charge.** According to the UVE, the charger can hold a far larger amount, sometimes more than double, and that money can stay unavailable for several days. If your available balance is low, bear that in mind.
:::

## How long it takes: it depends on four things {#quanto-tempo-demora}

There's no single time for everyone. According to the UVE, it depends on four things:

- **The car.** In AC, the on-board charger limits the power; in DC, the charging curve is what counts.
- **The battery level.** In DC, the highest power is usually between about 20 % and 80 %, depending on
  the model. With the battery very low, the car can take a while to reach maximum power. Near full it
  slows a lot: power usually drops sharply from about 80 %.
- **The charger's power.** What counts is the lower of the charger's power and what the car accepts.
- **The temperature.** Extreme cold and heat make charging slower. The battery charges best between
  about 20 and 30 °C; below 10 °C, the power can drop.

**The peak isn't everything.** A car that holds a high power for a long time can charge faster than
one that advertises a bigger peak but loses it in a few minutes, the UVE notes. For trips, look at
the 10 to 80 % minutes published for your car's version, not at the peak.

### The maths, for those who want to do it {#as-contas}

Do the maths with the numbers for your car's exact version, never from memory.

- **Power times hours gives energy**: 7 kW for 2 hours is about 14 kWh.
- **Approximate time** = energy to charge ÷ the lower of the charger's power and what the car accepts
  (the on-board charger in AC, the maximum power in DC).
- **It's an approximation.** In DC, the charging curve changes the result; in AC, losses count too.
- **"Kilometres per hour of charging" depends on consumption.** The same charger gives more kilometres
  to a car that uses less. So think in kW and kWh.

How many kilometres you get from what you charged is another sum: it's in [range and what changes
it](/autonomia/).

### Slow day to day, up to 80 % on a trip {#que-posto-usar}

- **Day to day**, a normal charger, while the car is parked anyway: at home, at work, at the shops.
- **On long trips or in a hurry**, a rapid or ultra-rapid charger.
- **On a trip, going to 100 % at a rapid charger doesn't save time.** As a rule it's better to go to
  about 80 % and move on to the next charger, the UVE recommends.

## Finding a charger, and checking before you go {#encontrar-postos}

There are maps and apps for that, according to the UVE: the MOBI.E map, Electromaps, PlugShare,
miio, the Tesla app and each OPC's own app. Some show the charger's status and comments from other
drivers.

Others bring together your car and your cards: they compare prices, estimate the time, tell you which
plug to use and plan the trip with its stops. The UVE mentions, for example, EVIO and miio in
Portugal, and Chargemap and Electromaps in Europe.

- **Before you go, check four things**: the plug, the power, the opening hours (some car parks close)
  and the OPC's support number.
- **No map guarantees a charger is free or working.** If the app shows real-time status, check how
  recent it is.

### No garage: Electric Streets {#ruas-eletricas}

For people who live somewhere with no parking space of their own, MOBI.E launched the **Ruas
Elétricas** (Electric Streets) project in 2024. Municipalities (câmaras) put forward residential
streets with no private parking, and MOBI.E hires OPCs to install and run low-power chargers (under
7.4 kW) on those streets. According to MOBI.E, it's the municipality that signs up, not the resident.

Want chargers on your street? Ask your câmara whether it has signed up or plans to. To find out which
streets already have them, look on the map.

## Charge with respect: the network belongs to everyone {#boas-praticas}

The public network is shared. The UVE sums up good manners in its "Carregar com Respeito" (Charge with
Respect) campaign:

- **Charge only what you need** to reach the next destination with a margin. In DC, leave when the
  power drops a lot, usually near 80 %. The last few percent cost many minutes, to you and to whoever
  is waiting.
- **Don't take a charging space without charging**, and move the car when you're done.
- **Plan.** Start a long trip with a full battery, avoid peak hours and, if there's a queue, stop a few
  kilometres earlier or at another OPC's charger.
- **There are no tickets in the queue.** Ask who's last and say how long you have left: that heads
  off almost every conflict.
- **Nobody has priority**, except emergency vehicles on duty.

Parking in a charging space without charging can get you a fine and a tow, the UVE warns. And a car
that isn't electric in a space reserved for electric vehicles while charging is punished under the
Código da Estrada, the Highway Code (DL 93/2025, art. 20.º, n.º 8).

**Check the idle fee before you walk away.** It's a price per minute that the OPC charges anyone who
leaves the car plugged in after the battery is charged. The OPC must display it at all its chargers
(DL 93/2025, art. 14.º, n.º 2).

## Before you plug in, the charger must show you the price {#o-que-o-posto-tem-de-mostrar}

At a public charger, before you start, you have the right to see (DL 93/2025, art. 14.º, n.ºs 1 a
3):

- the type of charger, the type of current and the maximum power;
- the ad hoc price with all its parts, per kWh, per minute or per session, including fees and taxes;
- the idle fee;
- the safety rules to follow.

Each unit also has two labels, according to the DGEG: one with the bodies responsible (the OPC, the
DGEG, ERSE and ENSE) and another with the charging instructions. If you don't know whose charger it
is, the label tells you.

More rules on your side:

- **The OPC must let you charge**, with or without a contract. It can only refuse for technical
  incompatibility with the car (DL 93/2025, art. 13.º, n.º 1, a), e n.º 2).
- **Public chargers are inspected** before opening and every 5 years by an inspection body (EIIEL).
  Without the certificate, ENSE can order the charger closed (art. 16.º).
- **To find out whether a company is a licensed OPC**, see the [list of OPCs](https://www.dgeg.gov.pt/pt/areas-setoriais/energia/energia-eletrica/mobilidade-eletrica/operacao-e-comercializacao/lista-de-opc/)
  the DGEG publishes, in Portuguese. From the old regime there's also the [list of CEMEs](https://www.dgeg.gov.pt/pt/areas-setoriais/energia/energia-eletrica/mobilidade-eletrica/operacao-e-comercializacao/regime-transitorio/ceme-registados/).
  The lists change: always check the DGEG's.

## Charger not working? Call the OPC, not MOBI.E {#quando-o-posto-nao-funciona}

Support comes from the charger's OPC, not from MOBI.E, the UVE points out. And the law requires the
OPC to have driver support available for the whole time the charger is in operation (DL 93/2025,
art. 13.º, n.º 1, k).

- **The number is on the charger's label and in the OPC's app.** The OPC publishes it, also on the
  National Access Point (Ponto de Acesso Nacional). Don't rely on a number from memory: numbers
  change. And the DGEG's lists of OPCs and CEMEs have no phone numbers.
- **Be wary of the phone number on maps.** A number that shows up on a map like OpenStreetMap may
  belong to the shop or the owner of the site, not to the charger's support.
- **Cable cut or charger vandalised?** Tell the OPC. According to the UVE, cable theft has mostly hit
  rapid chargers.

:::aviso
**Smoke, a burning smell or a bare cable: don't touch the charger or the cable.** Keep people away and call 112 if there's a risk. Many chargers have an emergency stop button: according to the DGEG, it's mandatory in installations above 22 kVA, but chargers installed before 2019 may not have one. Then tell the OPC, which must report any electrical accident to the DGEG and ENSE (DL 93/2025, art. 14.º, n.º 8).
:::

**To complain**, the OPC must have a complaints book (livro de reclamações). If there's no staff on
site, it must have the electronic book on its website and a notice at the charger saying how to
complain. Complaints are handled by ERSE (DL 93/2025, art. 14.º, n.ºs 6 e 7). What to gather is in the
next section.

## If the OPC doesn't sort it out, who to turn to {#a-quem-recorrer}

The OPC first, always. If it doesn't sort it out, it depends on the problem:

| Problem | Who | How |
|---|---|---|
| Invoice, price charged, poor service, an OPC that doesn't answer | ERSE, through a complaint | In the OPC's complaints book, on site or the [electronic one](https://www.livroreclamacoes.pt/). According to ENSE, the OPC must reply within 15 working days |
| Dangerous charger, uninspected or unlicensed; price or mandatory information not displayed; refusal to charge with no technical reason | ENSE, through a report | ENSE's [reporting form](https://www.ense-epe.pt/denuncias/), the email denuncias@ense-epe.pt or the free line 800 914 146 |
| Electrical accident or incident at a charger | 112, if there's a risk; then the OPC, which must report it to the DGEG and ENSE | ENSE has a line for accidents and incidents in the energy sector, 91 20 20 020, and the email acidentes@ense-epe.pt |
| Wrong data in apps and maps: a charger that doesn't exist, wrong availability or price | The OPC; the AMT, the mobility and transport authority, oversees data sharing | The data come from the OPC, through the EADME, the data aggregator, and the National Access Point |

How each charger's data reach the apps is in [the National Access Point and charger
data](/afir-nap/).

### Why it works this way {#porque-e-assim}

Who does what, under DL 93/2025:

- **ENSE supervises** compliance with the decree-law, with the AMT, without prejudice to ERSE and the
  security forces (art. 32.º). It decides the offences that aren't ERSE's or the AMT's (art. 37.º, n.º 3), such
  as failing on the information at the charger, on driver support or on universal access (arts. 13.º,
  14.º e 34.º).
- **ENSE can close a charger.** It closes at once a charger with no licence, no mandatory insurance or
  no periodic inspections (art. 33.º, n.º 1). It can close it, temporarily or permanently, for lack of
  safety, and ask E-REDES or the local distribution system operator (ORD) to disconnect it from the
  grid (art. 33.º, n.ºs 2 e 3). It receives the inspection certificates, valid for five years
  (art. 16.º).
- **ENSE investigates the accidents** the OPC reports to it and sends its findings to the DGEG
  (art. 14.º, n.ºs 8 e 9).
- **ERSE handles the complaints** from the OPCs' complaints books (art. 14.º, n.º 6) and breaches of its
  regulations and of the rules on unfair commercial practices (art. 37.º, n.º 1).
- **The AMT decides breaches** on charger data (art. 19.º and art. 37.º, n.º 2).

### What to gather for a report or a complaint {#o-que-juntar}

- the charger's ID and address;
- the OPC, which is on the label;
- the date and time;
- what happened;
- photos of the charger and of what was displayed.

ENSE's page on electric mobility still describes the 2010 regime. Don't go by it for today's rules.

## Plugs and sockets: the car's has to fit the charger {#fichas-e-tomadas}

When you travel, always check the charger's plug and the car's: they aren't all compatible. Adapters
exist, but they limit the power and aren't always safe.

### In Europe and Portugal {#fichas-na-europa}

- **Type 2 (Mennekes)**: the most common AC plug on European cars and chargers, up to 43 kW on
  three-phase. Most European cars use it; at home and at normal chargers it's usually the only plug.
- **CCS Combo 2** (Combined Charging System): the standard DC plug in Europe and the dominant one at
  rapid chargers. It combines the AC phases of Type 2 with two extra DC pins.
- **CHAdeMO**: the Japanese DC plug, still found on older cars (Nissan Leaf, Mitsubishi) and at some
  chargers. It's being phased out in Europe: new chargers and cars no longer include it. If your car
  is CHAdeMO, check before you go that the charger has one.

### CEE sockets: the colour tells you the power {#tomadas-cee}

Industrial CEE sockets (IEC 60309) are alternating current. You'll find them at slow public chargers,
campsites and private installations.

| Colour | Phases | Voltage | Current and power | Where |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Blue | single-phase | 230 V | 16 A (3.7 kW) or 32 A (7.4 kW) | Common at campsites and marinas |
| Red | three-phase | 400 V | 16 A (11 kW) or 32 A (22 kW) | Chargers and industrial installations |
| Yellow | | 110 V | | Rare in Portugal |

The red one gives the maximum AC power to cars with a three-phase on-board charger. For any of them
you need a cable of your own, with a CEE plug at one end and Type 2 at the other: many chargers have
no fixed cable.

### In North America {#fichas-na-america-do-norte}

The electrical system is different: 120 V / 240 V, at 60 Hz. The household socket (NEMA 5-15) can't
charge quickly. At home and at campsites people use the **NEMA 14-50** (240 V, 50 A).

- **J1772 (Type 1)**: the standard AC plug in the US and Canada, single-phase, up to 7.7 kW. Without an
  adapter, it isn't compatible with European Type 2.
- **CCS1 (Combo 1)**: the North American DC plug, the equivalent of CCS2 but with J1772 instead of
  Type 2. It was the DC standard in the US until recently.
- **NACS (the Tesla plug)**: Tesla's proprietary plug, the North American Charging Standard. Tesla
  opened it up and North American carmakers adopted it: today it's the de facto standard in the US,
  instead of CCS1. North American Superchargers use NACS, and cars with CCS1 need an adapter.
- **CHAdeMO** also existed in the US (Nissan Leaf), but it's being phased out.

### In China and Japan {#fichas-na-china-e-no-japao}

- **China: GB/T.** The Chinese standard, with its own AC and DC plugs, not compatible with CCS or
  Type 2. It's the predominant plug in China and in part of Asia.
- **Japan: CHAdeMO.** It's Japan's historical DC standard and still common on Japanese cars and
  chargers. In the rest of the world it's being phased out.

## Roaming and Plug & Charge: one card, many networks {#roaming-e-plug-and-charge}

- **Roaming**: an eMSP's card or app works at chargers of several OPCs, sometimes in several
  countries. Check with the eMSP which networks and countries it covers and what it charges for
  roaming: it can be more than the local price.
- **Plug & Charge**: with some cars and chargers you just plug in the cable, no card or app. It only
  works if the car, the charger and the eMSP all support it; otherwise, use the card or the app.

How this works under the hood (roaming hubs, OCPP, OCPI, the Plug & Charge certificate infrastructure,
batteries at chargers) is in [the technology behind charging](/tecnologia-de-carregamento/).

## Abroad, every country pays its own way {#no-estrangeiro}

Outside Portugal there's no single means of payment. Depending on the country and the network, you
pay by app, by bank card or with the network's own card, according to the UVE. The planning is up to
you. In the EU, the AFIR regulation requires public chargers to accept ad hoc payment.

Networks with their own app that the UVE suggests for trips:

- **IONITY**: rapid chargers, across Europe;
- **Iberdrola** and **Ibil**: Spain;
- **Fastned**: the Netherlands and Germany.

Electromaps covers the Iberian Peninsula. Check in the network's app before you leave: networks and
the countries they cover change.

## Electric or combustion: the comparison comes out every month {#custo-face-a-combustao}

There's no right number to know by heart. Every month the UVE publishes a [comparison of the cost of
driving 100 km](https://www.uve.pt/page/quanto-custa-100-km/) in electric and combustion cars, in
Portuguese. The [Electric Mobility Observatory](https://observatorio.uve.pt/) shows the average cost
of charging in Portugal. Always check which month the comparison is from. For the price of a specific
charge, use the tools at the end of the page.

## Myths and mistakes {#mitos-e-enganos}

- **"The charger's broken, I'll call MOBI.E."** No: support is the charger's OPC's job, the UVE points
  out. And the OPC must have it available for the whole time the charger is in operation (DL 93/2025,
  art. 13.º, n.º 1, k).
- **"No card, no app, no charge."** Chargers installed or renewed must accept ad hoc payment (art. 6.º,
  n.º 1), and the OPC must let any driver charge, with or without a contract (art. 13.º, n.º 1, a)).
  Only some chargers of 50 kW or more, already working on 19 August 2025, may not accept it until 1
  January 2027.
- **"At a rapid charger, any plug charges fast."** Through Type 2, the car charges in AC, limited by
  the on-board charger. Rapid charging goes through the DC plug, CCS or CHAdeMO, according to the UVE.
- **"A 150 kW charger always charges at 150 kW."** The useful power is the lower of the charger's and
  what the car accepts. And in DC it changes with the battery level and the temperature, the UVE explains.
- **"Charging to 100 % saves a stop."** At a rapid charger it doesn't save time: near full, the power
  drops a lot. As a rule, go to about 80 % and move on to the next charger, the UVE recommends.
- **"The car with the highest peak charges fastest."** Not always. A car that holds a high power for a
  long time can beat one that advertises a bigger peak but loses it in a few minutes, according to the
  UVE.
- **"Teslas have priority at Superchargers."** Not at the ones open to other brands, the UVE points
  out. On the public network nobody has priority, except emergency vehicles on duty.
